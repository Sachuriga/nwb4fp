REVIEWS:

Reviewer #1: The manuscript by Sachuriga et al. employs a viral-mediated intersectional genetic strategy to ablate CRs in the postnatal hippocampus. The authors propose that CR ablation induces transcriptomic and circuit-level alterations preferentially in superficial CA1 pyramidal neurons, while sparing deep CA1 neurons. Overall, the study provides potentially valuable insights into the role of postnatal CRs in hippocampal development and into the differential regulation of superficial versus deep CA1 pyramidal neurons.

However, the study is largely descriptive and lacks mechanistic insight. Although both the snRNA-seq and electrophysiology/in vivo imaging datasets suggest preferential effects on superficial CA1 neurons, these findings remain largely disconnected and do not establish a clear mechanistic relationship. In addition, it remains unclear whether CR ablation directly affects superficial CA1 neurons or whether the observed effects arise indirectly through alterations in other neuronal populations or network activity.

Several major concerns should also be addressed to strengthen the rigor and interpretability of the study.

Major concerns:

1.      To control for potential genetic/background effects arising from the Pde1c-Cre+ and Ndnf-flox-FlpO+ lines, the most appropriate control group for all experiments would be Pde1c-Cre+; Ndnf-flox-FlpO+ mice injected with a control AAV lacking DTA expression (e.g., EF1a-mCherry-FRT1-FRT2). 

2.      Although the authors demonstrate that their genetic ablation strategy reduces CR cell density in CR:DTA+ mice, potential off-target effects have not been adequately excluded. Such effects could arise from imperfect specificity of the Cre and/or Flp lines or from spontaneous recombination events during AAV production. One approach to address this concern would be to perform DTA immunohistochemistry shortly after viral injection, when DTA expression is expected to be maximal, to determine whether non-CR neuronal populations are also targeted. In addition, the authors should more thoroughly interrogate their snRNA-seq dataset to determine whether other hippocampal populations exhibit population-level changes suggestive of off-target effects. 

3.      Several concerns relate to the snRNA-seq experiments: 
a.      All other experiments appear to have been performed in adult mice, whereas the snRNA-seq experiments were conducted at P30. What is the rationale for this age difference? Given that the hippocamp is still maturing at P30, it is unclear how transcriptional changes observed at this stage relate to the electrophysiological alterations reported in mice older than 10 weeks. 
b.      The UMAP organization shown in Fig. S2 is unusual. Cell populations that are typically transcriptionally related appear widely separated (e.g., CA1/2 versus CA3 neurons, split interneuron clusters, and OPCs separated from oligodendrocytes). The clustering parameters and analysis pipeline should therefore be described in substantially greater detail. 
c.      Was any batch-correction method (e.g., Harmony) applied during integration? The UMAP distribution of CA1 neurons in Fig. 1A,B suggests potential batch effects and incomplete integration across samples. More broadly, many of the interpretations derived from the snRNA-seq data appear overstated relative to the evidence presented. For example, statements such as "These results suggest transcriptional consolidation, in that cell-type specific gene networks became more exclusive to their associated subtype," "Opcml network (S8) was non-significantly upregulated though ubiquitously expressed in all neuronal cell types, corroborating a decline in cell-specific regulation of transcription," and "Overall, these results indicate a high degree of alterations to a wide range of networks related to specialized neuronal functions including plasticity and connectivity" are not sufficiently supported by the data shown. 
d.      None of the snRNA-seq findings were experimentally validated. For example, the authors state that "Grm1 is a selective marker in CA1 superficial cells," implying subtype specificity of this network. Validation of Grm1 and/or other candidate markers, as well as confirmation of differential expression following CR ablation, would substantially strengthen the conclusions. 
e.      It would also be important to analyze transcriptional changes in additional cell populations, including granule cells and glia, to determine whether CR ablation induces broader network-level effects and whether the observed CA1 changes may arise indirectly through alterations in other cell types. 

4.      In the electrophysiological experiments, the identities of superficial versus deep CA1 pyramidal neurons should be validated post hoc using immunohistochemistry. 

5.      The authors previously reported that CR ablation alters dendritic spine density in CA1 pyramidal neurons. In light of the current findings, it would be important to determine whether CR ablation differentially affects spine density in superficial versus deep CA1 pyramidal neurons. 

6.      The electrophysiological data presented in Fig. 2C-L show similar directional trends in both superficial and deep CA1 neurons across several parameters. The apparent differences in statistical significance seem largely driven by variability rather than clearly distinct effects between the two populations. As such, the conclusion that superficial and deep neurons are differentially affected is not fully convincing. Relatedly, the manuscript should clearly report the number of animals and the number of recorded cells contributed by each animal for all electrophysiological analyses. 

7.      In Fig. 4O, there appear to be substantially fewer superficial CA1 neurons recorded in the experimental group compared to the datasets shown in Fig. 4N and Fig. 4E,F. The reason for this discrepancy should be clarified. 

8.      Were any behavioral phenotypes observed following CR ablation? If not, the functional significance of the reported transcriptional and circuit-level alterations remains unclear. 

9.      The Discussion is heavily centered on differences between superficial and deep CA1 pyramidal neurons, while the role of CRs themselves is comparatively underdeveloped. The section would benefit from restructuring to more clearly integrate the known developmental and circuit functions of CRs and to place the current findings within that broader context. 

Minor concerns:

1.      Line 146: "In response to +100 pA current injections, CR:DTA+ cells, especially superficial cells, appeared to be more excitable compared to their CR:DTA− counterparts." This interpretation is not obvious from the presented data and should be supported with quantitative analysis. 

2.      In Fig. S1G, the schematic would benefit from re-ordering for clarity. In addition, why are reelin+ interneurons largely absent in controls but present in the experimental group in Fig. S1E,F? The cells in Fig. S1F also appear larger, and the p73 signal appears less nuclear. Please ensure that image scaling is consistent and consider including DAPI staining to better demonstrate the expected nuclear localization of p73. 

3.      Fig. S1H reports a substantially higher density of CRs in the "outer blade" (which itself is not standard nomenclature for this region) relative to the fissure, which appears inconsistent with several prior studies. 

4.      The manuscript contains multiple typographical, formatting, and reference-formatting issues. Careful proofreading is recommended.


Reviewer #2 [Manuel Valero]: In this article, Sachuriga and collaborators investigate the role of postnatal hippocampal Cajal-Retzius cells in the maturation of CA1 circuitry. Using a genetic ablation strategy combined with single-nucleus RNA sequencing, in vitro electrophysiology, and in vivo recordings during spatial behavior, they show that postnatal CR cell ablation preferentially disrupts superficial CA1 pyramidal neurons. Specifically, the authors report transcriptomic alterations in gene networks related to synaptic and intrinsic neuronal properties, together with increased gamma oscillatory activity, altered intrinsic excitability, and impaired spatial representations, preferentially affecting superficial CA1 pyramidal cells.
        Overall, the study provides an interesting multi-scale characterization of how transient developmental populations contribute to the refinement of hippocampal subcircuits and their function in adulthood. The results are compelling and important for our understanding of hippocampal circuit development and function. However, in my opinion several aspects of the interpretation and methodological description would benefit from further clarification:

- Based on the analyses presented in Figure 3, the authors conclude that CR cell ablation enhances slow gamma activity, classically associated with CA3 inputs to CA1. However, neither the power spectrum (Figure 3A), nor the gamma amplitude-theta phase comodulograms (Figure 3E-F), nor the phase frequency-gamma amplitude cross-correlograms show a particularly clear slow gamma component. Given the highly localized and layer-dependent nature of slow gamma oscillations, it is also unclear how channels were selected for these analyses and whether channel selection was optimized for slow gamma detection (e.g. stratum radiatum). The manuscript would benefit from explicitly reporting the criteria used for channel selection and optimization for slow gamma detection. Otherwise, the relatively weak representation of slow gamma activity should be discussed more carefully in the interpretation of the results.

- Related to the previous point, in Figure 3 the authors report an increase in the number of slow gamma (and high gamma) events. This is a relatively non-standard approach for studying gamma activity and could potentially provide interesting information. However, I could not find in the Methods a clear description of how gamma events were detected and quantified. Curiously, the manuscript contains an explicit section describing ripple event detection, although no ripple data are presented in the study, while no equivalent methodological description is provided for gamma events. Did the authors use a similar detection approach for gamma activity? In any case, the criteria and procedures used for gamma event detection should be clearly described and justified.

- As the authors know, hippocampal ripples are strongly linked to CA3 driven activity and have also been reported to preferentially recruit superficial CA1 pyramidal neurons. Did the authors observe alterations in ripple activity, or in the participation of superficial versus deep CA1 cells during ripples, following CR-cell ablation? This could be particularly interesting in the context of the authors hypotheses and may help reconcile some of the gamma-related findings.

- While the manuscript emphasizes alterations in synaptic plasticity and connectivity, a substantial fraction of the transcriptomic changes appear related to morphology, cytoskeletal organization, and protein translation. It is therefore unclear whether synaptic dysfunction is indeed the predominant phenotype emerging from the transcriptomic analyses, or whether broader structural alterations may be equally or more strongly affected.

- More generally, the manuscript would benefit from a more explicit discussion linking the loss of postnatal CR cells with the observed transcriptomic alterations.

Minor comments:

- The F-I relationship of deep CA1 pyramidal neurons also appears to show a shift following CR cell ablation (although weaker than in superficial cells). However, I could not find a proper statistical analysis comparing the full F-I curves between groups for either superficial or deep cells (e.g. repeated-measures analysis or mixed-effects model).

- The Discussion is overall very good and thoughtful. However, it would benefit from a more explicit integration of the transcriptomic findings with the physiological and in vivo functional results.

- Given the strong transcriptomic signature related to morphology and cytoskeletal organization, the manuscript would benefit from including some quantitative morphological analyses of superficial and deep CA1 pyramidal neurons, which could help connect the molecular and electrophysiological findings.


Reviewer #3: Summary
The authors investigate the postnatal role of hippocampal CRs in the maturation of CA1 circuitry and spatial coding. Using neonatal CR ablation combined with snRNA-seq, ex vivo electrophysiology, in vivo LFP and single unit recordings, and place-cell analyses, the authors show that CR depletion selectively disrupts superficial CA1 pyramidal neurons. The study reports transcriptional dysregulation, increased intrinsic excitability, altered gamma oscillations, and impaired spatial coding specifically in superficial CA1 neurons, while deep CA1 neurons remain comparatively preserved. The authors propose that postnatal CRs contribute to the refinement and stabilization of hippocampal circuits beyond embryonic development. The manuscript is technically strong and combines multiple complementary approaches across molecular, cellular, circuit, and systems levels. The superficial-versus-deep CA1 comparison is particularly compelling and represents the conceptual core of the study.
The findings are potentially important for understanding developmental circuit maturation and selective hippocampal vulnerability. However, several limitations weaken the mechanistic and conceptual impact. Most importantly, the experimental evidence of a causal chain linking CR ablation to altered CA3 drive and impaired spatial coding is rather weak. In addition, the manuscript makes relatively strong claims regarding memory processing and disease relevance without behavioral validation or causal circuit manipulations. Some interpretations, therefore, extend beyond the presented data. Clarification of these issues and additional experiments would substantially strengthen the manuscript.

Major points
1.      One of my major concerns is that the proposed increase in the CA3->CA1 drive is not directly demonstrated. A central interpretation of the LFP analysis is that CR ablation enhances CA3 input onto superficial CA1 neurons, leading to altered gamma dynamics and degraded place coding. However, this conclusion is inferred indirectly from increased gamma power, increased theta-fast-gamma coupling, and altered superficial-cell excitability. Gamma bands are not uniquely pathway-specific and can also arise from local circuitry and mixed mechanisms (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24277864/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4290596/). I suggest directly measuring CA3-> CA1 connectivity by using pathway-specific optogenetics to analyze evoked responses or providing anatomical proof of altered connectivity by using monosynaptic tracing. At least, the Discussion should more clearly distinguish direct findings from mechanistic speculation.
2.      The authors discuss implications of their findings for memory functions and disease phenotypes. However, no behavioral assays are included, and the lack of behavioral validation limits the systems-level conclusions. Although superficial place-cell degradation is an important observation, it is unclear whether these physiological alterations produce measurable impairments in hippocampal-dependent behavior. The authors should either add additional experiments, showing that CR;DTA+ mice are impaired in spatial memory tasks, or moderate claims regarding memory processing and disease relevance.
3.      An open question remains the developmental vs. acute roles of CR ablation. CRs are ablated neonatally, meaning the manipulation affects hippocampal development throughout the postnatal maturation period. Consequently, it is difficult to distinguish ongoing functional roles of CRs from secondary developmental consequences of altered maturation. The observed transcriptomic and physiological changes could arise from broad developmental rewiring rather than direct CR-dependent modulation of mature circuitry. This limitation should be discussed more explicitly. An inducible later-stage manipulation or rescue experiment would help establish temporal specificity.
4.      Statistical and sample size reporting are very limited. For the snRNA sequencing data set, only 2 mice/group were used. Statistics were apparently performed on single nuclei without accounting for the effects of individual mice. For example, were the mice of the same sex? This could impact the gene expression profile. This is particularly relevant for hdWGCNA analyses, which can be sensitive to sampling variability. The authors should discuss this limitation and clarify how biological variability was controlled statistically. Moreover, in line 375 the authors state that data were statistically tested using Welch's t-test. Were the data tested for normal distribution? What statistical unit was compared: mice, recordings, or cells? For example, the legend in Fig. 3 states: n_CR;DTA- = 31, n_CR;DTA+ = 28. However, in Fig. S3, where the locomotion of the mice is compared, fewer data points are visible. Were the number of recordings used for statistics in Fig. 3? These issues
need to be clarified, and I strongly suggest the use of mixed-effect models for statistical comparison.
Minor points
1.      The authors state that spatial coding remains "intact" because field stability is preserved (i.e., line 222, 223). This interpretation is somewhat difficult to reconcile. Stability alone may not sufficiently support the claim of preserved coding quality. The authors should clarify what aspects of spatial representation are considered intact versus impaired.
2.      The authors should discuss how increased excitability and firing rates might impact their spatial coding analysis. If a neuron becomes globally more excitable, several apparent "spatial coding deficits" can emerge even without a primary impairment in spatial computation itself. Higher excitability can induce broader/noisier activity, resulting in larger place fields and lower information content. Therefore, a true spatial representation deficit must be distinguished from a simple increase in spike probability. Although spatial information was computed using a rate-normalized metric (bits/spike), several reported changes, including reduced information content, increased sparsity, and enlarged place fields, could still emerge secondary to increased intrinsic excitability and elevated non-specific firing. The use of a spatial memory test, as mentioned in point 2, could strengthen the biological relevance of their spatial coding analysis.
3.      Injections were performed at P0. At this age, stereotactic injections are very challenging, and the viral spread is great. The authors should quantify the spread into adjacent regions to analyze the impact on their results, or at least discuss this issue.
4.      In the method section, the authors write "LFP data were extracted from EEG recordings" (line 551). This sentence and the following sentences mentioning EEG analysis are misleading. EEG normally refers to scalp or surface recordings, not to intrahippocampal extracellular recordings using silicon probes. The authors should correct this imprecise terminology and rather write "LFP signals were extracted from broadband extracellular recordings".
5.      The authors applied a filter to remove 50 Hz noise. However, as shown in the power spectra, this filtering altered the spectral pattern. It therefore remains unclear how the filtering affects individual recordings. The authors should avoid considering this frequency range (i.e., 48-52 Hz) in their data comparisons.
6.      Supplementary Figures 2-4 do not have titles. The authors should add titles to improve clarity and presentation.
7.      The data representation is sometimes difficult to compare because the panels begin and end at different arbitrary values. The authors should standardize the representation across similar analytical methods and, ideally, use axes that start at 0



COMMENTS FROM THE ACADEMIC EDITOR:

This work addresses an important question regarding the long-term contribution of postnatal hippocampal Cajal-Retzius cells to mature circuit function and combines transcriptomic, electrophysiological, network-level, and spatial coding analyses in a coherent way. While several of the reviewers raise valid concerns regarding interpretation and support for some of the claims, I believe that many of these can be addressed through additional analyses, clarifications, and potentially targeted experiments. I would encourage the authors to focus their revision on reinforcing three aspects that appear central to the manuscript.

First, the specificity of the CR-cell ablation strategy would benefit from additional validation or discussion. While generation of entirely new control cohorts is not essential, the manuscript should more directly address the possibility of background or off-target effects and clarify the extent to which the reported phenotypes can be attributed to CR-cell ablation itself.

Second, the evidence supporting selective effects in superficial CA1 pyramidal neurons should be strengthened, as this is a central pillar of the manuscript and underlies much of the biological interpretation. The goal is not necessarily to provide exhaustive additional characterization, but rather to increase confidence that the reported effects are indeed preferentially associated with the superficial CA1 population.

Third, several aspects of the transcriptomic analyses would benefit from further clarification, including the logic of the analytical framework, statistical treatment, and limitations of the dataset. The conclusions derived from these analyses should be clearly supported by the level of replication and resolution available.

More generally, I would encourage the authors to focus the revision on strengthening support for the principal claims of the manuscript rather than expanding the scope of the study. Ultimately, the key issue for this Short Report is not whether it provides a complete mechanistic dissection of the underlying circuits, but whether the current data convincingly support persistent and selective effects of postnatal CR-cell ablation on mature CA1 circuit function.
